Added sugar vs. natural sugar: what is the difference?

A gram of glucose is chemically the same molecule whether it came from fruit or a syrup. But foods are not just molecules: source, structure, fiber, serving size, and whether sugar was added during processing change the nutritional context.

By AvoKindPublished September 19, 20264 min readSources checked September 19, 2026
The short answer

On U.S. Nutrition Facts labels, “Total Sugars” includes both naturally occurring sugars and added sugars. “Added Sugars” is the subset introduced during processing or packaged as sweeteners, including sugars from syrups, honey, and concentrated fruit or vegetable juices used as sweeteners. Naturally occurring sugars in intact fruits, vegetables, and milk are not counted as added sugars. The distinction is useful because foods containing natural sugars can also provide fiber, protein, micronutrients, and a different food matrix.

Key takeaways

  • Total Sugars = naturally occurring sugars + added sugars.
  • Added Sugars are listed separately on U.S. Nutrition Facts labels; the FDA Daily Value is 50 grams on a 2,000-calorie diet.
  • “No added sugar” does not mean “sugar-free.” Fruit and some vegetables naturally contain sugars.
  • Health effects depend on dose, food form, and dietary pattern—not simply whether a molecule of sugar is “natural.”

What does “added sugar” mean on a food label?

The FDA defines added sugars as sugars added during processing, sugars packaged as sweeteners, sugars from syrups and honey, and sugars from concentrated fruit or vegetable juices when they function as sweeteners. They do not include sugars naturally present in intact milk, fruits, and vegetables.

The “Includes X g Added Sugars” line sits underneath Total Sugars because added sugar is part of the total—not a separate pile added on top of it.

What are Total Sugars?

Total Sugars is exactly what it sounds like: all sugars in the serving, regardless of whether they were naturally present or added. A plain piece of fruit has total sugar but zero added sugar. A sweetened fruit yogurt can contain both lactose from milk and added sugar from a sweetener.

Does “no added sugar” mean sugar-free?

No. A product can have no added sugars and still contain naturally occurring sugars from fruit, vegetables, or dairy ingredients. That is why reading both Total Sugars and Added Sugars is more useful than relying on a front-of-pack phrase.

Is natural sugar chemically different?

Not necessarily. Glucose, fructose, and sucrose are the same molecules regardless of source. What changes is the food around them. Whole fruit contains water, fiber, micronutrients, and plant structure. A sweetened beverage can deliver a similar sugar dose with very little structure and can be consumed quickly.

This is why nutrition research tends to evaluate foods, beverages, and dietary patterns rather than declaring every gram of sugar nutritionally identical in practice.

How much added sugar is recommended?

The FDA Daily Value for added sugars is 50 grams per day on a 2,000-calorie diet, and U.S. dietary guidance recommends limiting added sugars to less than 10% of total calories. The Daily Value is a labeling reference, not a personalized prescription.

Why do public-health guidelines focus on added sugar?

Added sugars can increase energy intake without necessarily adding the fiber, protein, vitamins, or minerals found in whole foods. High intakes are also common in sugar-sweetened beverages, desserts, candy, and other foods that are easy to consume in large amounts.

That does not mean every food with added sugar is harmful or that every naturally sweet food is unlimited. The category is useful because it helps people see how much sweetness manufacturers added on top of the food’s original ingredients.

What about fruit juice and fruit concentrates?

Labeling depends on how an ingredient is used. The FDA counts sugars from concentrated fruit or vegetable juices as added sugars when those concentrates are used as sweeteners. One hundred percent juice has its own labeling and dietary-guidance context and should not be treated as nutritionally identical to intact whole fruit.

Good label question: “How much sugar is in one serving, and how much of that was added?” That is usually more informative than arguing whether all sugar is “good” or “bad.”

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between total sugar and added sugar?

Total sugar includes all sugars in a serving. Added sugar is the portion introduced during processing or provided by added sweeteners.

Does fruit sugar count as added sugar?

Sugar naturally present in intact fruit does not count as added sugar. Concentrated fruit juice used as a sweetener can count as added sugar under FDA labeling rules.

Does no added sugar mean sugar-free?

No. A food with no added sugar can still contain naturally occurring sugars from fruit, vegetables, or dairy.

What is the Daily Value for added sugar?

The FDA Daily Value is 50 grams per day based on a 2,000-calorie diet. U.S. dietary guidance recommends keeping added sugars below 10% of total calories.
