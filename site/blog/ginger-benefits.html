Does ginger have health benefits? What human studies actually show

Ginger has one of the better human research records among culinary herbs, especially for some types of nausea. But most clinical studies test capsules or extracts—not the small amount of ginger used to flavor food.

By AvoKindPublished September 19, 20264 min readSources checked September 19, 2026
The short answer

Human studies suggest ginger supplements can help some forms of nausea—especially pregnancy-related nausea—and may affect menstrual pain or selected metabolic markers. The evidence is not equally strong for every claimed benefit, and many trials use concentrated supplements at doses far above normal culinary intake. Eating ginger as food can be part of a varied diet, but it should not be marketed as if a pinch of ginger reproduces a supplement trial.

Key takeaways

  • The clearest human evidence for ginger is around some forms of nausea, although results vary by setting.
  • Most clinical research uses standardized powders, capsules, or extracts rather than ordinary culinary amounts.
  • Large reviews report promising findings across several outcomes but also substantial differences in study quality and preparation.
  • Ginger supplements can cause gastrointestinal side effects and may interact with medications; food use is not equivalent to high-dose supplementation.

What part of the ginger plant do we eat?

The part commonly called ginger root is technically a rhizome—an underground stem of Zingiber officinale. It has been used as food and spice for centuries and contains pungent compounds such as gingerols and shogaols.

That culinary history is real. It is also separate from the question of whether concentrated ginger supplements treat specific symptoms.

Where is the evidence for ginger strongest?

The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) concludes that ginger may help nausea and vomiting associated with pregnancy. Evidence is less clear for motion sickness, postoperative nausea, and chemotherapy-related nausea, where studies differ in design and quality.

A comprehensive systematic review of 109 randomized controlled trials found reasonably consistent support in areas including pregnancy-related nausea and some metabolic or inflammatory outcomes, while many other proposed effects remained controversial. An overview of systematic reviews likewise found promising evidence but emphasized heterogeneous and often limited methodological quality.

Does ginger “settle the stomach”?

That phrase is broad, but it points to the area with the most clinical interest. Ginger has been studied for nausea in pregnancy, after surgery, during chemotherapy, and in other settings. Different causes of nausea are not interchangeable, so a positive result in pregnancy cannot automatically be applied to motion sickness or cancer treatment.

For anyone dealing with persistent or severe nausea—especially during pregnancy, chemotherapy, or after surgery—food advice should not replace medical care.

What about inflammation and metabolic health?

Recent reviews of ginger supplementation report changes in some inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers. These findings are scientifically interesting, but two cautions are essential: first, biomarkers are not the same as clinical outcomes; second, the trials generally use defined supplement doses rather than a small culinary amount.

For a food brand, the responsible translation is “ginger contains bioactive compounds and has been studied in humans,” not “ginger in this product reduces inflammation.”

Is fresh ginger the same as ginger extract?

No. Drying, extraction, heating, and storage change the relative amounts of gingerols, shogaols, and other compounds. Supplements can also deliver grams of ginger powder or concentrated extracts in a consistent dose. A thin slice of fresh ginger in a meal is a different exposure.

Evidence rule: when a study uses a supplement, report it as a supplement study. Do not silently translate the result into a claim about culinary ginger or a multi-ingredient food.

Is ginger safe?

Ginger has been used safely in many clinical studies, but oral supplements can cause abdominal discomfort, heartburn, diarrhea, or mouth and throat irritation. NCCIH advises people taking medications to discuss herbal supplements with a clinician because interactions are possible.

Why does AvoKind use ginger?

Mostly because food should taste like food. Ginger brings aroma, warmth, and a bright spicy edge that helps balance greens and fruit. Its research history is interesting, but we do not need a medical claim to justify its place in the formula.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best-supported benefits of ginger?

Human research is strongest for some types of nausea, particularly pregnancy-related nausea. Evidence for other uses varies in quality and consistency.

How much ginger do studies use?

Many clinical trials use measured ginger powders, capsules, or extracts, often at gram-level daily doses. Those exposures are not equivalent to normal culinary use.

Can ginger reduce inflammation?

Supplement trials report changes in some inflammatory biomarkers, but results vary and biomarker changes do not prove that culinary ginger treats inflammatory disease.

Can ginger supplements interact with medications?

Potential interactions are possible. NCCIH advises people who take medicines to discuss herbal supplements with their health care provider.
